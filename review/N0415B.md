## Look-back window

**7 days**[^1]

7-day look-back period, or since admission/entry or reentry if the stay is less than 7 days. The item table on p. N-4 states the window as "during the last 7 days or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days." The manual uses "7-day look-back period" (N0415B1, p. N-7) and "7-day observation period"/"observation period" (Coding Instructions, p. N-6; N0415B2, p. N-7) interchangeably for the same 7-day window.

## Coding rules

1. Column 1 (Is taking): check if the resident is taking any medications by pharmacological classification, not how it is used, during the last 7 days or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days.[^4]
2. Column 2 (Indication noted): if Column 1 is checked, check if there is an indication noted for all medications in the drug class.[^3]
3. Code all high-risk drug class medications according to their pharmacological classification, not how they are being used.[^5]
4. Column 1: check if the resident is taking any medications by pharmacological classification during the 7-day observation period (or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days).[^6]
5. N0415B1: check if an anxiolytic medication was taken by the resident at any time during the 7-day look-back period (or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days).[^1]
6. N0415B2: check if there is an indication noted for ALL anxiolytic medications taken by the resident any time during the observation period (or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days).[^2]
7. Code medications according to the medication's therapeutic category and/or pharmacological classification, not how it is used; e.g., oxazepam prescribed for use as a hypnotic is categorized as an antianxiety medication and is coded as antianxiety, not as a hypnotic.[^7]
8. Medications with more than one therapeutic category and/or pharmacological classification are coded in all categories/classifications assigned to the medication, regardless of how the medication is being used.[^8]
9. Include any of these medications given to the resident by any route in any setting (e.g., at the nursing home, in a hospital emergency room) while a resident of the nursing home.[^9]
10. Code a medication even if it was given only once during the look-back period.[^10]
11. Count long-acting medications given every few weeks or monthly only if they are given during the 7-day look-back period (or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days).[^11]
12. For transdermal patches, only capture the medication if the transdermal patch was applied to the resident's skin during the observation period.[^12]
13. Combination medications should be coded in all categories/pharmacologic classes that constitute the combination.[^13]
14. Herbal and alternative medicine products (e.g., melatonin, chamomile, valerian root) are considered dietary supplements and should not be counted as medications.[^14]
15. Example application: lorazepam 1 mg PO QAM received every day for bipolar disorder is coded N0415B1 and N0415B2 checked — lorazepam is an antianxiety medication and the indication of use (bipolar disorder) is noted.[^15]
16. N0415Z1, None of the above, is checked if none of the listed medications (including antianxiety) were taken by the resident at any time during the observation period (or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days).[^16]

## What the chart must show

1. Review the resident's medical record for documentation that any of these medications were received by the resident and for the indication of their use during the 7-day look-back period (or since admission/entry or reentry if less than 7 days). *(importance to an auditor: high)*[^17]
   Step 1 of Steps for Assessment; establishes both the receipt of the medication and the indication as record-documented facts.
2. Review documentation from other health care settings where the resident may have received any of these medications while a resident of the nursing home (e.g., valium given in the emergency room). *(importance to an auditor: high)*[^18]
   Step 2 of Steps for Assessment; the manual's own example is an antianxiety medication.
3. An indication is the identified, DOCUMENTED clinical rationale for administering a medication, based upon a physician's (or prescriber's) assessment of the resident's condition and therapeutic goals. *(importance to an auditor: high)*[^19]
   Definition governing whether Column 2 (N0415B2) may be checked.
4. Assessors should be able to identify the source(s) used to support coding the MDS 3.0 pharmacological classification; facilities may wish to identify a consistently used resource. *(importance to an auditor: medium)*[^20]
   Framed as a suggestion ("may wish") but with an expectation that the classification source be identifiable.
5. Assessors should consult the manufacturer's package insert, which may contain the medication's pharmacological classification, and may work with the resident's pharmacist to confirm classification(s). *(importance to an auditor: low)*[^21]
   Suggested source of classification evidence.
6. When a resident receives antipsychotic, antianxiety and hypnotic medications simultaneously there must be a clear clinical indication for their use; adequate documentation is essential in justifying their use. *(importance to an auditor: high)*[^22]
   Stated in the note following the N0415 example; the manual adds that administration in this combination could be interpreted as chemically restraining the resident.
7. Providers are responsible for coding each medication's pharmacological/therapeutic classification accurately; the listed resources are informational only and their use is not required for MDS completion. *(importance to an auditor: medium)*[^23]
